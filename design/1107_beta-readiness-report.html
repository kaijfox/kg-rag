Does the rebuilt kg-rag work? Evidence for three uses

Written 2026-11-06 after a rebuild of the system and a round of beta preparation. Every number below was produced by a script in experiments/, and the raw output sits beside the script. The runs use one laptop, local models (embeddinggemma for search; qwen3.5 for extracting facts, answering and judging) and small corpora. They show whether each part behaves as designed. They do not estimate how well it will do for a tester.

1. Papers

The test material is four physics textbooks, two by each of two authors (Kardar: 223 passages; Plischke: 89), with 63 reviewed questions. Each question was written from one or more specific passages, which are its answer.

Does the rebuilt search return what the first build returned?

Why it mattersThe rebuild replaced a 14-package system. Every measurement made on the first build is only worth keeping if the new plain search returns the same passages.

What was doneBoth textbook databases were rebuilt from the kept parsed documents, and the 63 questions were searched. Recall at k is the share of a question's answer passages among the first k results.

What to see: each pair of bars is the rebuilt system and the first build, and they are the same height everywhere. Recall was identical at k = 1, 3 and 5 for 63 of 63 questions (baseline-parity.out). Why it matters: an unchanged baseline means the earlier evaluation sets remain valid and any change we measure later comes from a new layer.

JudgementThis shows no regression, and nothing else. The questions were written from the passages and so share their vocabulary, which favours a similarity search. Even so, the right passage is first for only 57% (Kardar) and 68% (Plischke) of questions.

Do the retrieved passages improve an answer?

Why it mattersRecall counts passages. What an agent needs is a better answer. A retrieved passage could be the wrong one and still score as a hit, or the right one and go unused.

What was doneTwelve Plischke questions (8 single-passage, 2 multi-passage, 2 whole-topic) were answered by the local model in three conditions: no passages; the answer passages themselves (the best a search could do); and the five passages that plain search returned. The same model then scored each answer from 0 to 1 against a reference answer.

score 1score 0.5score 0
What to see: each row is a question, each column a condition. The left column (no passages) has four cells that are not green: two red and two amber. Three of them turn green in the middle column (search results); the amber whole-topic cell stays amber. The right column, with the correct passages, is green throughout. The number at the right is recall at five for that question. Means: no passages 0.75, search results 0.96, correct passages 1.00.

JudgementThe direction is right and the gain comes from questions where the model did not know the answer. Four things limit it. (1) Twelve questions: one question moves the mean by 0.04 to 0.08. (2) The scores come in three steps. (3) The judge is the model that wrote the answers. (4) Thermodynamics is well known, so the model scores 0.75 unaided; a private corpus would show a larger gap. Two further observations: the two multi-passage questions were already answered correctly without help, so this test says nothing about multi-passage retrieval, and in the seventh row the answer scored 1 in every condition although the search returned none of the answer passages (recall 0), so the model knew the answer without them.

Does the facts layer, the default for a new database, improve retrieval?

Why it mattersA new database extracts short standalone facts from every passage with a model and ranks them beside the passages. That costs a model call per passage, so it should beat plain search or it should not be the default.

What was doneBoth Plischke textbooks were built with extraction on: 89 passages gave 530 facts and 1,725 name mentions. The build took 677 s, with model responses from an earlier trial's cache available for 39 of the 89 passages. All 26 Plischke questions were then searched with plain search and with the facts machine (facts-and-neighbours), retrieval only. A fact counts for the passages it cites.

What to see: the two bars are close at every k. The facts machine is ahead at k = 1 (0.686 against 0.679) and 3 (0.865 against 0.840), and behind at 5 (0.865 against 0.878). Question by question at k = 5, it did better on 1 question, worse on 2 and the same on 23. Why it matters: differences this small, on 26 questions, are noise.

JudgementThis test gives no reason for the facts layer to be the default, and no reason to think it hurts. It is not a fair test of the layer's purpose, which is questions that combine several passages or ask about a whole topic: only 4 multi-passage and 2 topic questions exist here, and on the topic questions the facts machine was lower (top-five recall 0.50 against 0.67, averaged over two questions). The log trial below shows one cost of the layer that this test does not: facts can fill the result slots.

2. Project log

Why it mattersA project log contains decisions that later decisions cancel. A plain search for "where is the data kept?" returns the old and the new answer side by side, and an agent cannot tell which one holds. The log search is meant to return only the entry that still stands. It learns that an entry was replaced from a sentence in the newer entry, such as "This supersedes the decision of 2026-03-02", found by a text rule with no model involved.

What was doneI wrote a 16-entry log for an invented ingestion service in the format the beta guide teaches an agent. It contains seven true replacements: five written in the sentence form the rule reads, and two written the way a person might write them without having read the guide. Ten questions each have one entry as the right answer; seven of them have an older, replaced entry that a plain search would also find. Both searches ran on real embeddings with fact extraction on, as a new database is set up (99 to 121 s to build; 46 facts).

What to see: in the plain search the replaced entry is ranked above the current one. Because the OAuth entry states the replacement, the log search never returns the API-keys entry. Why it matters: an agent reading the first result would have acted on a decision that no longer holds. This is the case the feature exists for.

JudgementThe mechanism works and the damage is visible. Three failures stand out.

  • Silent misses. A replacement stated without a date is invisible to the rule and leaves no log line. The guide tells an agent to name the earlier entry's date, so a log written to the guide is covered, and a log written without it is not. A tester has no way to know which they have.
  • Backwards passive. "X of [old date] is replaced by this" is a natural way to write a replacement in the newer entry, and the rule reads it backwards. The rule could check which entry is the newer one and flip the reading; it does not.
  • Search misses are separate. For the first and third questions the current entry is not returned by either search. Every entry in this toy log begins "The ingestion service…", so that phrase dominates the match: the log search's three results for the first question are facts that the log is written in JSON. With facts on, 22 of the 30 result slots held a fact, and in several rows two or three slots held near-duplicates of one entry (the OAuth example above shows it). A real log has less shared wording, but the slot crowding would remain.

Without fact extraction the log search returned the current entry in the top three for 9 of 10 questions; with it, 8 of 10. This is one run on one invented log that I wrote together with its questions and knew the rule's format. Rates here describe this log; they are not estimates for a real one.

3. Code map

Why it mattersAn agent working on a codebase needs to find which file does a thing. The design uses what developers already write: documentation comments above functions. A documentation tool turns them into a folder of web pages, and kg-rag reads the pages as they are. kg-rag has no code-specific parts, so the quality depends on the pages.

What was doneTwo tools, two codebases. TypeDoc built a site from this repository's TypeScript (50 files; 256 pages, 674 passages), and twenty questions about the code, each with the file that answers it, were searched. pdoc built a site from the Python sidecar (591 lines, 8 modules) with ten questions. A search succeeds at k when one of the top k results is a page about an answering file or module.

What to see: TypeDoc as it comes finds the answering file in the top five for 15 of 20 questions (75%). When the comment at the top of each file gets a @module tag the figure rises to 17 of 20 (85%), though the first-result rate slips from 13 to 12. pdoc finds all 10 with or without its embedded source. Caution: the pdoc site without source is 11 passages, so five results cover almost half of it and 10 of 10 is an easy result.

What to see: the source blocks multiply the index five times and add half again to every answer, with nothing gained in finding the module. The beta guide now says to pass the flag.

Judgement

  • Where the five TypeDoc misses come from. In this repository the explanation of how a file works is the comment at the top of the file, and TypeDoc drops that comment unless it carries @module. The function pages of runMachine, createViewerServer, main and MACHINES held only signatures. Tagging recovered two questions. Three are still missed (the viewer, the list of search machines, the runner) and I do not know why; the viewer file's page now has its comment.
  • A weaker result than the route this replaced. The earlier route, which converted TypeDoc data into markdown with a file-and-line marker, found 19 of 20 on the same questions and returned about 1,200 characters per search, against 1,578 now. It could also name the line. The new route is general and has no converter, and it costs those points.
  • Limits of the test. The questions were written by the code's author and mostly reuse the code's own words. The credit goes to a module, not to a function. The comments here are uneven. Sphinx, which the guide describes as coarse, was not measured in this report. Nothing compares against searching the raw source files, which the earlier route matched.

4. What was not tested

  • Real agents on real work. No agent has used the tools to do a task. The server process was exercised end to end by a script earlier today (searches, usage reports, a watched file re-read, 7 searches compared with the baseline in kg report); that run is recorded in design/measurements.md and was not repeated here.
  • A fresh install. Nobody has followed the setup guide on a clean checkout. The sidecar's own Python tests pass (37, 1 skipped), but PDF parsing with docling was not run in this report; the textbook runs used kept parsed copies.
  • Hard questions. Multi-passage and whole-topic questions number 6 of 26 in the facts test and 2 of 12 in the answer test. The facts layer was designed for them and was not tested on them.
  • Large corpora. The largest index here holds 223 passages. Search speed, extraction time and cache size at thousands of passages are extrapolated from small runs.
  • Upgrades and moving extraction. Rebuilding from the model-call cache and moving the cache to a second database were measured earlier (4.9 s against 1,122 s; 0 model calls on the second database) and not repeated here.
  • Independence. The log, the code questions and the toy scenarios were written by the people who wrote the system; the judge and the answerer are one model. A tester's corpus may behave differently.

5. Is it ready for a beta?

For a small beta with testers who know it is early: yes. The core path works and reproduces. Plain search is unchanged from a measured baseline, retrieved passages helped an answering model on a small sample, replaced decisions in a project log stop appearing when the log follows the documented format, and a generated documentation site is searchable with a general HTML reader.

Three things should be said plainly in the beta guide.

  • The facts layer is on by default and no test here shows that it helps. The shadow log of plain search beside it is how the beta settles that.
  • The project-log feature protects only entries whose replacement is stated with the earlier entry's date; otherwise it fails without a message.
  • A code map is as good as the comments in the generated pages. TypeDoc needs @module to include file-level comments, and pdoc needs --no-show-source.

Two defects are cheap to fix before testers arrive: a replacement the rule reads backwards could be flipped when only one order is possible, and an entry that says "no longer used" without a date could be reported as a possible replacement instead of being ignored.
